# Readings

Constraint: seven sessions, students at the start of a 1-year MA, three graded paper
commentaries. Keep **one assigned item per session**, short, and with no statistics the
students can't skip past.

## Assigned — one per session

### Session 1 — Introduction
- Hannah Fry, ["What Data Can't Do"](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2021/03/29/what-data-cant-do),
  *The New Yorker*, 29/03/2021. **[keep]** — ungraded, sets tone.
- *Optional:* Matthew Salganik, [*Bit by Bit*](https://www.bitbybitbook.com/) ch. 1
  ("Introduction"). Free online, ~15 pages. Worth assigning because it frames the whole MA.

### Session 2 — Questions and data provenance — **[changed]**
- David Lazer, Ryan Kennedy, Gary King & Alessandro Vespignani (2014),
  "The Parable of Google Flu: Traps in Big Data Analysis," *Science* 343(6176): 1203–1205.

  **Why this one:** three pages, zero equations, and it carries the whole session — drift,
  measurement validity, big-data hubris, and the platform as part of the DGP. Graded
  commentary #1.

  *Discussion prompts:* What was GFT actually measuring? Whose behaviour changed — the
  public's, or Google's? Would more data have fixed it? What would you have had to do in
  2009 to notice this coming?

- *Optional companion:* Deen Freelon (2018), "Computational Research in the Post-API Age,"
  *Political Communication* 35(4): 665–668. Two pages, and directly relevant to students
  about to propose scraping something.

### Session 3 — Measurement and description
- Carl Müller-Crepon, Philipp Hunziker & Lars-Erik Cederman (2021),
  ["Roads to Rule, Roads to Rebel"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022002720963674),
  *JCR* 65(2–3): 563–590. **[kept, moved from session 2]**

  **Why here:** it is fundamentally an operationalization paper — a relational concept of
  state capacity constructed out of road networks. In the measurement session the discussion
  becomes "what concept, what operationalization, what would you have done instead," which is
  a much better use of it than a general discussion of quantitative research. Graded
  commentary #2.

  *Discussion prompts:* What is the concept, in one sentence, without using the word "roads"?
  What does the road network fail to capture? Tell me a story where their measure goes up and
  actual state capacity goes down.

### Session 4 — Causality
- Andrew Guess et al. (2023),
  ["How do social media feed algorithms affect attitudes and behavior in an election
  campaign?"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abp9364), *Science* 381(6656): 398–404.
  **[keep]**

  Graded commentary #3. Students can skip the statistics entirely and still discuss the design,
  which is the point.

  *Discussion prompts:* What exactly was randomized, and what wasn't? Who consented, and how
  does that bound the estimate? If the effect is near zero, what have we learned — about feeds,
  or about three months? Where does SUTVA fail here?

### Session 5 — Designs, generalization, credibility
- **No assigned reading.** Protect the time for essay questions; this is the last session
  before the workshop.
- *Optional:* Salganik, *Bit by Bit* ch. 6 ("Ethics"). Free online.

## Instructor background — not assigned

Useful for building the new blocks; too long or too technical for this cohort.

**Data provenance / CSS design (session 2)**
- Matthew Salganik (2018), *Bit by Bit: Social Research in the Digital Age*, Princeton UP.
  Ch. 2 ("Observing Behavior") is the source for the ten-characteristics framework. Free at
  bitbybitbook.com. **The single most useful book for this reframing.**
- Wang, Rothschild, Goel & Gelman (2015), "Forecasting elections with non-representative
  polls," *IJF* 31(3): 980–991. — the Xbox study.
- Timnit Gebru et al. (2021), "Datasheets for Datasets," *CACM* 64(12). — good source for a
  "what you should be able to say about your data" checklist slide.

**Measurement (session 3)**
- Justin Grimmer, Margaret Roberts & Brandon Stewart (2022), *Text as Data*, Princeton UP.
  Ch. on validation; the "no free lunch" principle.
- Ziad Obermeyer et al. (2019), "Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the
  health of populations," *Science* 366(6464): 447–453.
- Dean Knox, Christopher Lucas & Wendy Cho (2022), "Testing Causal Theories with Learned
  Proxies," *Annual Review of Political Science* 25. — background for the
  classifier-output-as-variable slides; the intuition is teachable, the paper isn't.
- Raj Chetty et al. (2022), "Social capital I," *Nature* 608: 108–121.
- Michel et al. (2011), "Quantitative Analysis of Culture Using Millions of Digitized Books,"
  *Science* 331: 176–182; and Pechenick, Danforth & Dodds (2015), "Characterizing the Google
  Books Corpus," *PLoS ONE* 10(10).

**Estimands and theory (session 4)**
- Ian Lundberg, Rebecca Johnson & Brandon Stewart (2021), "What Is Your Estimand?" *ASR*
  86(3): 532–565. **Strong fit for this course's philosophy** — it's about connecting a
  theoretical question to a defined target quantity, and most of it needs no statistics.
  Consider assigning excerpts in a future year, or lifting its central diagnostic ("state your
  estimand in words") as an essay requirement.
- Christopher Bail et al. (2018), "Exposure to opposing views on social media can increase
  political polarization," *PNAS* 115(37): 9216–9221.
- Nyhan et al. (2023), "Like-minded sources on Facebook are prevalent but not polarizing,"
  *Nature* 620. — companion to Guess et al. from the same research programme.

**Prediction (sessions 1 and 5)**
- Jake Hofman, Amit Sharma & Duncan Watts (2017), "Prediction and explanation in social
  systems," *Science* 355(6324): 486–488. Two pages; the cleanest statement of the distinction.
- Jake Hofman et al. (2021), "Integrating explanation and prediction in computational social
  science," *Nature* 595: 181–188.
- Matthew Salganik et al. (2020), "Measuring the predictability of life outcomes with a
  scientific mass collaboration," *PNAS* 117(15): 8398–8403. — Fragile Families Challenge.
- Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan & Obermeyer (2015), "Prediction Policy Problems," *AER P&P*
  105(5): 491–495.

**Credibility and ethics (session 5)**
- Nate Breznau et al. (2022), "Observing many researchers using the same data and hypothesis
  reveals a hidden universe of uncertainty," *PNAS* 119(44).
- Kramer, Guillory & Hancock (2014), "Experimental evidence of massive-scale emotional
  contagion through social networks," *PNAS* 111(24): 8788–8790.
- Michael Zimmer (2010), "'But the data is already public': on the ethics of research in
  Facebook," *Ethics and Information Technology* 12(4). — the Tastes/Ties case.
- Egami & Hartman (2023), "Elements of External Validity," *APSR* 117(3): 1070–1088. **[already
  cited in deck 5]**
- Kevin Munger (2023), "Temporal Validity as Meta-Science," *Research & Politics*. **[already
  cited in deck 5]**

## Textbook list on the syllabus

Current list (Huntington-Klein, Imai, Toshkov, Cunningham) is fine and should stay. One
addition is worth making explicit for this cohort:

- **Salganik, *Bit by Bit*** — free online, no statistics, and it is the only one of these
  books that is *about* computational social science research design rather than about
  estimation. Given the MA's focus it arguably belongs first on the list.

Note that Imai and Cunningham are both considerably more technical than this course; worth a
one-line annotation on the syllabus saying which books are for *this* course and which are
for the methods courses that follow. Students at the start of a master's read a booklist as
a set of instructions.
